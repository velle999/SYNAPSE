The desktop release. 0.2.5 rebuilt the installer; this one rebuilds what you
land in afterwards. Fifteen settings panels were keyboard-only, the control
panel was a flat list of twenty-one rows, and Alt+Tab switched windows without
showing you any of them.

## The settings panels take the mouse

Every compositor-drawn panel — the control panel, the wallpaper picker, the
task manager, the theme manager, the clipboard, the mixer, all fifteen of them
— was keyboard-only. They said "Up/Down select · Enter activate" and meant it
literally, and none of them closed when you clicked off, which is the one thing
every menu on every desktop does.

They now work under one contract: **hover selects the row under the cursor, a
left click does that row's primary action, a click off the panel closes it, and
the wheel scrolls.** Every click routes through the panel's own activate path,
so the mouse and the keyboard cannot come to disagree about what a row does.

The deliberate exceptions are all about not doing something destructive by
accident. The task manager's click only *selects* — kill stays on the keyboard.
The wallpaper and cursor pickers and the news panel take a **double** click to
commit, because moving their selection applies a wallpaper, swaps a cursor
theme or opens a browser.

The bar's right-click menu and the mixer now dismiss with a real popup grab
rather than a pointer-leave timer, which is what "the bar menu won't close" was
about. Escape works on them too.

## One control panel instead of twenty doors

Super+C listed the settings, but as a flat column of twenty-one rows with
toggles and jump-offs interleaved — and every jump-off was a one-way door.
Opening Displays from the control panel and pressing Esc dropped you on the
desktop rather than back where you came from.

It is now shaped the way a settings app is shaped: **a category list down the
left** (Appearance, Desktop, Display, Sound, Network, Power, System,
Shortcuts), that category's rows on the right, Left/Right and Tab between the
two, and **Esc stepping back a level** before it closes anything. Opened by
their own keybind the same panels still close straight to the desktop.

One table now declares every setting, which closed two real gaps: **nine
settings the compositor owns had no row anywhere** (Cursor theme, Date & time,
Night light, News, Clipboard history among them), and the start menu's Settings
page was a second hand-written list that had drifted out of sync with the
first. Both now read from the same table, so they cannot disagree about what
settings exist.

**Desktop ▸ Layout leads that category** — it reads `tiling · desktop 1`, and
Enter cycles the same four layouts Super+Tab walks. The layout was reachable
only from a key you had to already know existed, on a panel that is meant to be
the front door. The row names the desktop because a layout belongs to one
workspace rather than the whole session; without that it would look like a
global setting that mysteriously disagrees with itself every time you switch
desktops. It fires the same action the key does, so it inherits the
reclaim-on-tiling behaviour below and cannot drift away from it.

`synctl dispatch control display` opens straight onto a category.

## Alt+Tab shows the windows it is switching between

Alt+Tab has walked most-recently-used order for a while, but blind — on a
workspace of four terminals that is four identical flashes and you tab past the
one you wanted. It now puts **a grid of live window thumbnails** on screen
while Alt is held, the target outlined in the accent colour with its full title
along the bottom.

The tiles are the clients' own buffers handed to the scene graph, so the
compositor copies no pixels and does no GPU work of its own — which is what
makes a grid of eighteen live windows affordable. More than a page pages the
grid, a window with nothing committed falls back to its app icon, and
`alt_tab_preview = off` in `synuirc` keeps the cycle and drops the picture.

**And it reaches every window you have open, not just the ones in front of
you** — windows on your other virtual desktops and minimized ones are in the
cycle too, each tile saying which desktop it is on. The window you actually
want is as often somewhere else as it is on screen, and a switcher that only
shows what is already visible makes you remember where you put everything,
which is the job it exists to do for you.

Letting go of Alt on such a window switches to its desktop, or restores it.
That happens once, at the end of the cycle rather than as you tab past — so
passing over three minimized windows on the way to a fourth does not bring all
three back, and crossing two desktops does not cross-fade the desk twice before
you have chosen anything. `alt_tab_all_desktops` and `alt_tab_minimized` narrow
it back if you would rather Alt+Tab stayed where you are.

## Tiling that stays tiling — Super+Shift+T

A tiling desktop could quietly end up with nothing left to tile. Dragging a
window to move it, snapping it to a screen edge and maximizing it all mark the
window "floating", **every layout skips floating windows**, and the only thing
that ever cleared that again was Super+F, one window at a time. So one drag
took a window out of the tiler for the rest of the session, and the desktop
looked like a tiler that had stopped working. It had not — it was laying out an
empty set.

The part that made it feel unfixable: maximizing and un-maximizing records the
floating flag on the way in and puts it back on the way out, so on a window
that is *already* floating it restores the floating. Trying to fix a stuck
desktop by re-maximizing could never work, by construction.

**Super+Shift+T now puts every window on the desktop back in the layout** —
un-maximized, un-snapped, un-floated — and tiles it. It works from any layout,
switching a floating desktop to tiling on the way, because a key called "tile"
that visibly does nothing is the same bug one level up. Dialogs and fullscreen
windows are left where they are.

**And selecting a tiling or AI layout now reclaims the desktop by itself**, so
choosing the layout means "place these windows" rather than "place whichever of
these windows nothing has floated yet".

Super+Shift+T was the calendar, which did not need it — clicking the clock in
the bar opens the calendar, and that is how everyone reached it.

## Laptop lids

The lid belonged to logind alone: closing it suspended, and changing that meant
editing a root-owned file. Three new rows on the Super+P power panel —

| | |
|---|---|
| **docked** | a non-built-in monitor is connected (default: ignore) |
| **plugged in** | on mains or USB power (default: suspend) |
| **on battery** | (default: suspend) |

— each set to `system`, `ignore`, `blank`, `lock` or `suspend`. `blank` turns
the built-in panel off and leaves your external monitors alone, which is the
case the docked setting exists for; `system` hands the lid straight back to
logind, and synui asks logind what it would have done rather than guessing.

Which one applies is decided **at the moment the lid shuts**, so plugging a
monitor in or unplugging the charger changes the answer with nothing having to
notice. The defaults match the previous behaviour, so nothing changes unless
you ask it to, and if synui dies logind takes the lid back — the failure mode
is the old default, never a laptop that no longer sleeps in a bag.

## KDE Connect

Pair a phone with the desktop — notifications, clipboard, file transfer, remote
input. It is on the ISO and offered by the installer as an optional group,
declinable on a Minimal install for the same reason the file manager is (it
pulls the KF6 tree in on its own).

Almost none of the theming this looked like it needed was needed: **three of
its four UI surfaces are already SynapseOS's.** Phone notifications go through
the notification daemon synui itself serves and draws, the indicator is a tray
item the bar already hosts, and the window chrome is synui's like any client's.

## Bluetooth headsets that connect at 100% volume

Some headsets announce maximum volume the moment they connect, overwriting the
volume that was just restored — full blast into your ears, every time. PipeWire
fixes this per device and ships the quirk for a dozen units; this adds the ones
it does not carry yet, including the Soundcore P20i.

The database it lives in is owned by another package and has no drop-in
directory, so a **pacman hook regenerates our copy from the packaged one**
whenever either package moves, rather than freezing upstream's quirk list at
whatever shipped the day you installed. Every failure path disarms the redirect
and falls back to the packaged database, because the one outcome worse than the
bug is losing every quirk upstream ships.

## Fixes

- **Tiling had nothing left to tile.** A window's remembered geometry included
  a floating flag, and one drag to a screen edge wrote `floating=1` down as a
  preference — after which the window reopened floating, closed floating, and
  wrote it back out again. Every layout skips floating windows, so a desktop
  set to tiling gradually became a desktop where the tiler ran on an empty set.
  A layout that places windows itself now owns their geometry; floating and
  monocle keep the whole feature.
- **Monocle hid the window you had just focused.** The monocle layout shows one
  window per screen and hides the others, but it only ever chose which one when
  something reflowed the desktop — and nothing on the focus path did. So
  Alt+Tab, Super+J/K, the dock and an ordinary click all handed the keyboard to
  a window that stayed invisible, on top of the one still being drawn: you were
  typing into something you could not see. It corrected itself the moment
  anything else reflowed the desk — a window opening or closing, a desktop
  switch, Super+F — which is most of what you would try next, and is why it
  went unnoticed for so long. The reveal now happens where focus changes, so
  every way in is covered at once.
- **A window could shrink inside its own borders** — a maximized Firefox with
  its frame at full size and the page rendered into 552x304 in the corner, the
  desktop showing through the rest. A client that missed a configure kept the
  size it picked for itself forever. The compositor now notices and says it
  again, with guards so a terminal snapping to its character cell is not
  mistaken for one ignoring us.
- **Backdrop blur distorted on rotated monitors.** A landscape panel turned on
  its side sampled the wrong part of the buffer, correct in a band across the
  top and broken on a hard edge below it. Upstream's fix, backported.
- **D-Bus-activated GUI services could not start at all.** Nothing published
  the display to the user service manager, so anything launched that way came
  up with no display, found no fallback and crashed — the caller seeing only
  "unit failed", naming neither the display nor the cause. KDE Connect's daemon
  is what found this; nothing about it was KDE Connect-specific.
- Qt applications were configured against a widget style this distro does not
  ship. Only the widget shapes were ever wrong — the colours land by a
  different path — but they land on a style that is actually installed now.
- Super+Tab said what it did. It was firing all along on desktops where every
  window was floating, and moving nothing.
- Firefox's Alt+Tab tile came out an empty plate: it keeps its toplevel as a
  bare frame and paints the whole window into a subsurface.

## Installer and disk encryption

- **"Enable GPU inference? yes" never enabled GPU inference.** Putting
  inference on the GPU replaces the CPU build rather than installing alongside
  it, and `--noconfirm` answers pacman's replacement question with its default,
  which is *no*. So the command could not succeed under any circumstances:
  every NVIDIA install answered yes and then ran the whole reason this distro
  exists on the CPU. The AMD/Intel branch had the identical bug. Both now
  answer only that question, with a fallback path, and put the working build
  back if the swap leaves nothing installed.
- **The keyboard question was answered in the wrong namespace.** The console
  and the desktop resolve keymap names against different databases, and they
  disagree on **four of the fifteen offered languages**. A UK or Japanese user
  got the console they asked for and a US desktop; a Brazilian or Korean user
  got exactly the reverse — on a machine whose keyboard is now wrong, which is
  the worst possible state to go read a log from. Both halves failed silently,
  which is why it survived. The two are separate answers now, each checked
  against its own data files before it is written.
- **`syn-crypt` told an encrypted disk it was not encrypted.** Two independent
  faults in how it resolved the root device covered *every* encrypted layout
  the installer can produce — a btrfs root (i.e. every encrypted install that
  chose snapshots) reported NOT ENCRYPTED, and any other one resolved to a
  device name with a box-drawing character in it. So adding a key, changing a
  passphrase or backing up a header has never worked without naming the device
  by hand, on a tool the installer's closing screen points you at.
- The ADVANCED path now catches a partition named twice **at the prompt**.
  Nothing was ever corrupted by it — every tool involved refuses a mounted
  device — but the failure landed after the confirmation and after the root had
  been formatted, reported as "Failed to format /dev/sdaN". At the prompt it
  costs a retype.

## Testing

The synui test suite passes clean under AddressSanitizer, which it did not
before. Of the four failures, three were not leaks in synui at all: the
headline "1322 bytes leaked in libdbus" was the graphics driver's own
connection held for the process lifetime, and one whole test was reading a leak
in its *own* code as a security regression — it checks that a sandboxed client
is denied the privileged screen-capture and keylogging interfaces, and any
non-zero exit reads as "it was not denied". The sandbox was fine; 20 of 20
assertions pass.

The suite is thirteen tests, eight of them new this release: the pointer
contract above and its hit-testing, the resize heal (a real client that deliberately drops a
configure), the lid decision table including the on-battery branch,
geometry-versus-layout, reclaiming a floating desktop back into the tiler,
Alt+Tab's reach across desktops and minimized windows, and the monocle
invariant — exactly one window visible per screen, and it is the focused one.
Plus the first tests `syn-crypt` has ever had.
