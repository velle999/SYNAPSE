The desktop grows its own furniture.

This release is about the things you look at all day: the bar, which now takes
third-party widgets in a format nine hundred of them are already written for;
the dock, which you can resize, restyle, and hang a clock and a power button
on; the desktop, which has widgets of its own; and the welcome screen,
which stopped being a list of nineteen labels and became a guide.

Underneath that is a run of bugs with one shape — **text that was present and
could not be read**. A dim grey chosen once against one background, an accent
measured off a wallpaper and then drawn on that same wallpaper, two contrast
correctors that both returned early on exactly the surfaces they exist for.
Each was invisible in a screenshot of the theme it was written against and
plainly wrong on the next one.

Also here: the lights in the machine follow the wallpaper, GeForce NOW ships,
and MangoHud stopped loading itself into every Vulkan program on the system.

## The bar takes plugins

A bar plugin is a directory with a `manifest.json` and some QML. SynapseOS
reads **Omarchy's** format rather than inventing a second one — their desktop is
a long-lived quickshell process in which the bar and the panels are all plugins,
and this bar is quickshell too, so theirs is the only format that already
describes "a QML widget you can drop into a quickshell bar". A widget written
once loads on either. `~/.config/omarchy/plugins` is searched *first*, so
something installed with `omarchy plugin add` is found without being copied.

**The browser browses nine hundred of them, not five.** The first cut shipped a
hand-written catalogue, so the window, the TUI and `synui-plugins browse` were
all faithfully showing a list nobody could grow. `synui-plugins refresh` fetches
the community catalogue now; two dozen of the entries are games — Tetris, Snake,
Minesweeper, 2048, Wordle, solitaire — which is most of what somebody opens a
widget browser hoping to find, and they get a Games row of their own.

**Then three widgets installed off that browser went on and never appeared**,
with the browser saying "installed and on" for every one. The browser was not at
fault: synui's `qs.Ui` module had two types and Omarchy's has thirty-two, so most
of the nine hundred were written against a module this desktop did not have — and
a QML property that does not resolve is not an error, it is zero. `qs.Ui` and
`qs.Commons` are *implemented* now, over SynapseOS's own theme: the same type
names and contracts, drawing this desktop's font, spacing and ink rather than
theirs. Measured against 40 of the most-installed community widgets: **9 of 40
resolved before, 39 of 40 do now.**

**A plugin is more than a button.** Half of them put their actual behaviour in a
panel that opens under the button, or in a background service — and both were
being read and then dropped, so a plugin whose whole point was its panel got a
bar button that did nothing when pressed, with no error, because every caller
guards. Panels and services are hosted now, mounted once per session rather than
once per monitor, so a widget with a score does not run three simulations racing
over it. The five `omarchy-*` commands their widgets call by name ship as shims,
and `jq` — which every one of them assumes — is a dependency instead of a
coincidence.

**The bar's own right-click menu grew a Plugins section**: a checkbox per plugin
and arrows to reorder them, so the two things you do to a widget after installing
it do not require a second window. The first version of that reorder cost two
full catalogue scans and a reload of every widget on the bar for one click; the
bar states the order itself now.

## The dock, and what is on it

The dock and the bar had drifted into two different ideas of where a setting
lives. They agree now, and the dock gained the settings it never had: **it
resizes**, the magnify swell is a number rather than a mood, and it stops
floating over windows that should be in front of it.

**Things on the dock move.** The clock, the apps button and the power button can
each be dragged to a different cell — a clock that could not be moved was the
report that started it, and the apps button was opening Rofi rather than the
application page its own picture is a picture of. A button is its own label.

**The apps button opens a real application page**, which takes the mouse and has
no letters left stranded on it, and there is now one place that says what the
start menu is rather than three that disagreed.

**The dock's marks ask what they are landing on.** The clock, the apps grid and
the power mark were washed out on Prism because they were drawn in a fixed ink
against a dock whose transparency the theme decides. They measure their own cell
now — not the whole strip, which is a different question with a different answer
on a dock that is half over a window.

**And its icons go monochrome with the desktop.** A wallpaper with no colour in
it answers in white and grey now instead of inventing a hue from noise; the dock
follows that answer, so a greyscale desktop is greyscale all the way down.

## Widgets on the desktop

The desktop right-click menu can reach them now, and there is an **analog clock**
among them with four faces — minimal, a railway dial, roman numerals, and a neon
one with a glow ring. It is frameless and resizes by its corner. It deliberately
does not cycle its face on a click: a widget that takes clicks is one the desktop
right-click menu cannot be opened through, which is a price the launcher and the
note pay on purpose and a clock has no business paying.

**The dock's own clock did not fit a vertical dock.** A horizontal bar's clock
cell is measured — cairo is asked how wide the strings are and the cell grows
along the run until they fit — but a column's run is its *height*, and its width
is the dock thickness, which nothing the clock can do will change. The old code
gave up and drew a 15px time into a flat 40px cell, so `12:34:56 PM` ran off both
edges. The digits fit now (the font size comes down until they do, with a floor),
and there is an analog face for the dock as well, which is the arrangement that
cannot overflow at all: a dial is square, and a column has exactly that many
pixels each way.

Its test suite had been green the whole time, because that rig stubs text
measurement to zero on purpose — so every string in it is 0px wide and a clock
cell can never overflow. The new one draws with real text and reads the pixels
back, asserting the property rather than an image: no bright pixel in the margin
at the edge, at three dock thicknesses, with the widest string the clock can make.

**The music widget can start the music.** Every row in its drawer was drawn and
then refused: the rows named verbs the widget had no way to reach. It picks a
source and plays now, draws a YouTube playlist's tracks *by name* rather than as
a column of rows all reading `watch`, and fetches the cover art for them.

Two bugs that only appear when you make the dial bigger, both from a number that
was right at exactly one size. Its hands turned into slabs — 46 pixels wide where
15 was meant — because the widths were written as a fraction of the radius *and*
then passed through the scaler, so they grew with the **square** of the clock.
And the rim between the dial and the edge of its box was a flat 6 pixels, chosen
when the outermost neon glow pass reached 5 past the radius; at the 420px ceiling
it reaches 15, so nine pixels of glow ring had nowhere to go and the face came
out as a circle with four flat sides.

## The welcome screen is a guide

It was the first thing a new SynapseOS desktop showed, and it was a *menu*: one
513-pixel column, nineteen labels and their chords, drawn in cairo by the
compositor. Nothing on it said what any row was for, so "Neural Overlay" and
"Cat Mode" arrived side by side and the only way to find out what either did was
to press it.

It is six pages now — Welcome, The keys, Make it yours, The AI, Everything else,
You're set — with a rail down the left that doubles as a contents page and a
description under every row. `synui-welcome` opens it from a terminal,
Super+Escape still toggles it, and there is a **Welcome Guide** entry in the
applications menu, because a panel reachable only by a chord is a panel nobody
finds.

It is its own process rather than a window in the bar, and that is deliberate:
two bars ship, and a guide living inside one of them would not exist for anyone
running the other. As a second entry point into the same QML tree it still gets
the theme, the picked UI font and the glass namespace without copying any of
them, and dismissing it ends the process.

**Its key column is still the live bind table**, which took a new command to
keep. 0.2.9.3 stopped the old menu hand-typing its chords — they had been wrong
before, because the command bar has been on Super+Space, on Super+= and back —
but it could do that by reading the compositor's table directly, being *in* the
compositor. A guide outside it cannot. So `synctl binds` reports that table, with
each chord already spelled the way the shortcut palette and the control panel
spell it, and a rebound key is right in all three without anybody editing a
string.

## Text you could not read

**Panel text was illegible on glass, and both correctors were sitting out.**
Reported as the emoji picker's unhighlighted categories and the keyboard
shortcuts panel. Measured on stock Prism with a panel over a white web page: the
surface composites to L=0.135, and on it the accent landed at 1.52:1, the hint
line at 1.26:1 and the count at 1.70:1 — while the panel's own body ink cleared
4.75:1. One root cause, and it is a shared first line: both correctors open by
returning early on a pale surface, because both were written when a panel was the
theme's own opaque colour. A glass panel is not.

**The dim ink was a grey from memory.** One value for dark themes and one for
pale, chosen once against one popup background. It is the second line of every
widget — the artist under the track, the date under the clock, `9.4 / 31.3 GiB`
under the memory bar, the label on every menu row that is switched off — and it
measured **1.85:1** on the popup this desktop actually draws, and under AA on all
fifteen shipped themes: 1.36:1 on Nord, 1.55:1 on Dracula, 1.58:1 on Tokyo
Night. It is computed against the surface now, so no theme has to remember to
ship a grey for it.

**A widget's accent was corrected against nothing.** Its ink already went through
the contrast walk; its accent did not. That is invisible on a widget whose marks
are drawn in the ink and total on one whose marks *are* the accent — the neon
clock face draws its bezel, glow ring, twelve ticks, both hands and the pin in
the accent and not one stroke in the ink, so a wallpaper whose accent sits near
its own luminance drew a clock the colour of the sky behind it, at 3.0:1. The
music widget's artist line and progress fill are the same case and took the same
fix.

Big screen mode had its own version of this, and the arcade's headline had been
landing on the shelf underneath it.

**And half the bar stopped inverting.** The weather and the volume in white, the
CPU meter, the Bluetooth glyph and the network glyph in black, side by side on
one strip — identical code reading identical properties. Nothing was wrong with
the ink rule; what was wrong was the picture it was asked about. The bar decides
which way its ink runs per module, and for a stretch of bar no window covers, the
only per-column answer it had was a cell of the desktop-wide luminance grid — a
ninth of the screen deep, standing in for a bar 34 pixels tall. On a photograph
with a dark canopy along the top edge over lit leaves below it, that cell reads
0.29 where the bar's own strip reads 0.08, and those are opposite sides of the
flip. synui measures and publishes the strip itself now, column by column.

## The lights

`syn-rgb` reads the colour this desktop already chose and hands it to OpenRGB.
synui has always measured the wallpaper and written an accent that the bar, dock
and icons wear; everything else in the room that lights up was still whatever
somebody else's software had set it to.

It can follow the wallpaper's accent, the theme's accent or a fixed colour;
brightness scales the channels rather than moving the hue; `dark` turns the
lights off without turning the bridge off. The watching is systemd's — a `.path`
unit on the two state files, so there is no daemon and nothing running between
one wallpaper and the next. It ships **off** and is enabled per user with
`syn-rgb on`, because it writes to hardware, and hardware already doing what its
owner asked is not a thing to take over on an update. Control panel ▸ Appearance
has the switch, beside the wallpaper accent it follows.

Four bugs found by using it. The bridge was **nine seconds behind** and dropped
whatever happened in the gap. The board's lights **went out a few seconds after
login** — and the RAM's did not, which is what made it findable. Clicking
through the wallpaper picker quickly **killed the lights for the rest of the
session**. And the last one had been there from the first day: **only the RAM
followed the wallpaper**, while the board and the keyboard sat on whatever
colour they were already holding. syn-rgb caches which devices are on the bus,
and that cache can only ever be smaller than the truth — a device that has gone
away fails the push and gets noticed, a device that has arrived fails nothing at
all. At login it is always built short: the DIMMs answer OpenRGB in half a
second and the USB board and keyboard take nearly eight, and the bridge runs one
second in.

## Games

**GeForce NOW ships**, as `syn-gfn`. Not the Electron client: measured against
geforcenow-electron 3.0.2, it cannot do either of the two things a game stream
is, and both failures are silent. Its Wayland branch hardcodes a GL
implementation Chromium 142 removed, so the GPU process dies at every launch and
the software fallback never repaints at the size the compositor gives it — synui
sends `configure(2556, 1382)`, the client acknowledges exactly that, and then
paints 800×600 into the corner with the desktop showing through the rest. And it
binds the pointer-constraints protocol and never calls `lock_pointer`, so the
cursor leaves the window mid-game and lands on the next monitor. Neither is
synui's doing: a compliant client driven through the same sequence in a nested
headless synui fills the output exactly.

Every hard part of a cloud-gaming client — pointer lock, keyboard lock,
fullscreen, H.264/HEVC decode, WebRTC — belongs to a browser engine that has
already been written and already tested against this service. So `syn-gfn` is a
launcher: the first Chromium-family browser it finds at runtime, in a profile of
its own, with keyboard lock and pointer lock pre-granted for the site because the
permission prompt they replace is raised while the page is fullscreen with the
cursor captured, where nobody can see it. No browser is pulled in as a
dependency.

**Big screen mode gets it too**, plus a **Recent bar** and a mouse wheel that
actually browses. The Recent bar is recorded by the launcher itself rather than
by the shell, so no way into a tile can forget to remember it, and it stores ids
and looks them up at draw time, so a tile whose program has gone is simply not
drawn and returns to its place if it comes back.

**And the desktop remembers what it has opened.** The Recent bar was reported
empty on a machine that had been in use all day, because it was counting what had
been launched *from the television*. A launch can happen from the start menu, the
dock, the app grid, a desktop icon, a file manager, a terminal or another
program; recording it in one of those is a list that disagrees with reality
within a day, and recording it in all of them is six copies of one rule. Every
launch has exactly one thing in common — a window turns up — so that is where it
is counted now.

**MangoHud is no longer exported for the whole session.** Its Vulkan manifest
declares `enable_environment MANGOHUD=1`, so that one variable loaded the overlay
into *every* Vulkan client on the system: a game, a browser, a video player, any
QML application that touched QtMultimedia, a test. On AMD the layer segfaults the
client inside its own device-creation hook and on NVIDIA it never does, which is
why it had already taken down the live wallpaper, Studio and Studio's own test
suite on one machine while every run on another passed — and each fix was another
opt-out in another launcher. The overlay comes from the launcher now, which is
the only place it can be turned on anyway: `syn game …` wraps it, and
`syn game hud on` puts the old behaviour back for a machine that has never seen
the crash.

## Getting around

**The shortcut palette is a hotkey manager.** It listed the bind table, so the
only rows it had were the chords that already existed — which made removing a
shortcut a one-way door: unbind a chord and the row naming it is gone from the
table the list is derived from, and the way back was to hand-edit `synuirc`. The
list is "what a key could go on" now: bound actions, *unbound* ones, and your
installed applications.

A bug that came with it: giving a terminal application a key wrote the bare
`Exec=` line, so pressing that key started `htop` with nothing to draw into and
it died in the same instant. Nothing on screen, nothing in the log, and only for
`Terminal=true` entries — every GUI application was bound correctly, which is why
the row looked like it worked. The start menu had always wrapped those entries;
the palette built the command a second time and did not. There is one builder
now.

**The machine can be renamed.** Every SynapseOS install answers to `synapse`, so
two of them on one network means Avahi renames one `synapse-2.local` — with no
say in which and no promise the suffix survives a reboot, which is a `.local`
address nobody can rely on. Renaming was a `hostnamectl` one-liner, which is to
say it was not in the settings application at all. Settings ▸ System has the row
now, validated where it can say what was wrong with what you typed rather than
where it can only say "exited 1".

**The control panel is regrouped.** It had grown a row at a time and the headings
had stopped describing what was under them — the clock had *four* homes, and
"Dock" was one 22-row heading holding the dock, the start menu, a widget setting
and the clock. The rule the table is ordered by now, and says so at the top: a
row goes with the thing it is **about**, not with the thing that implements it.

## Fonts

**One font family for the whole desktop.** It had two owners: `font.state`,
written by `synui-apply-font` and read by the bar, the file manager, settings,
disks, the updater and the software manager — and a second copy inside synui's
own config, applied to everything the compositor draws. So changing the font from
any window in the suite moved every application on the desktop and left the dock,
the control panel, the notifications and the window titles on the previous face
until the next login. The compositor's copy is retired.

**And there is now something in the picker.** Both synui's font picker and
Studio's title-font list are plain fontconfig queries — they show what is
installed. A stock install carried Noto, DejaVu and a console face, so choosing
the font the desktop is drawn in was a choice between DejaVu and Terminus, and
lettering a title meant typing a family name into a field and hoping. Fifteen
families ship with every install now, sans, serif and mono, all OFL or Apache.

## The house desktop ships assembled

Prism has been what a fresh install boots into for two releases, but only the
*theme* was ever written down — everything the theme is for was left at compiled
defaults, so the desktop SynapseOS looks like was six control-panel rows somebody
had to find and dial in themselves. The installer and the live image now write
the whole thing: glass on, transparency at Prism's own levels, a floating pill
bar, a capsule dock, widget glass, the accent measured off the wallpaper, menus
inking off the window behind them, and St. Louis at night under all of it.

Every one of those is written as `auto` rather than as a number, which took two
tries. The first pass spelled out the levels because `auto` did not resolve to
the desktop Prism is drawn for — a workaround, and one that cost exactly what
`auto` exists to prevent: an explicit level survives a theme switch, so anyone
who later picked Gruvbox was handed Prism's glass. The two real gaps are closed
now (the panels' ladder, and the dock having no theme-supplied answer to fall
back on at all), so the numbers are simply redundant and are gone.

There is a fourth stock wallpaper — the Gateway Arch at night — and a noir cut
of the same photograph.

## Studio

The video side gained the half an editor is actually driven from. A **source
monitor and three-point editing**: the cut had one viewer and one way in, where a
whole file landed at the end of a track and was trimmed afterwards. An editor
decides the in and the out on the footage first, puts the playhead where it goes,
and lets the third point follow.

**A curve over time** — the darkroom has had a curve widget over tone since the
beginning, and this is the same idea over duration, which is the last thing
keyframes were missing. A key could be dropped and nudged, but a move over four
seconds was a list of numbers rather than a shape.

**A smooth cut**, which is a morph across a jump cut with every frame between two
shots invented by motion estimation, and a neural noise-reduction model for
dialogue. **A thumbnail maker** in the darkroom — canvases listed by destination
rather than by aspect ratio, because nobody has ever wanted 16:9, they have
wanted the one YouTube takes. Projects can be given a name, the darkroom has undo
and keys, and dragging a photograph out of it works — carried by the pointer
rather than by Qt's drag, which stopped starting one at all in 6.11.2.

## Software, files and text

**The software manager searches every source at once.** `synpkg search --all`
asks the repositories, BlackArch, the AUR and Flathub the same question and
prints one table with each row labelled by where it came from; the window gains an
"All sources" tab above the three single-source ones.

**And it draws icons for software you have not installed.** Every step of the icon
chain asked the local disk — the icon theme, then the installed `.desktop` files —
so each could only answer for software already on the machine, and the suggested
list is 105 applications most of which are by definition not installed. Measured:
15 of 104 resolved, and all 15 were installed. It reads the AppStream catalogue
now, which is what a distribution ships for exactly this. Separately, icons are
looked up by the *application's* name rather than the package's, which is a
different string more often than it sounds.

**The file manager's Open With flyout closed before you could click it.** Qt
delivers the hover enter/exit pair to exactly one item, so a mouse area filling a
popup is exited the instant the pointer reaches a row inside it — which armed the
dismissal with the pointer resting on the menu. The bar's own right-click menu and
the volume mixer had the identical bug and took the identical fix. A rename could
also silently take the extension off a file.

**Right-clicking in the text editor typed into the buffer.** It repositioned the
caret with raw motion keys before opening its menu, with no insert-mode guard, so
in INSERT the motion itself went into the document — which reads as right-click
Paste inserting garbage. The same release makes the editor start in a mode that
takes typing, and gives its window a drop target, which it did not have at all.

## Under the hood

**The application grid froze the desktop for a third of a second every time it
opened.** Not the `.desktop` scan (6ms for 361 files) and not the SVG rasterizing
(22ms for a page): the icon lookup enumerated themes × roots × sizes × categories
and probed a path for each combination — **23,040 `access()` calls for a single
icon**, on the compositor's main thread. A page of 24 tiles is 395,498 syscalls.
Almost all of it was proving the same absences over and over: six themes against
eight roots, of which seven pairs exist. The directories that exist are indexed
once now.

`tools/preflight.sh` gained a check for a component that was only *built* looking
like one that was *edited*, which is the difference between an update that ships
and one that silently does not.

## Known issues

- Bar plugins are read in Omarchy's format, and plugins that reach past it into
  `Quickshell.Hyprland` cannot run here — the browser says so on the row rather
  than letting one install and do nothing.
- Studio's video timeline is complete and tested but still has no window of its
  own; it is driven from `synstudio timeline`. The photo darkroom is the GUI that
  ships.
- The ISO's `/usr/share/synapseos/logo.svg` is still the violet cut alone —
  unchanged from 0.2.9.3 — so anything that falls back to it gets one washed-out
  mark on a pale theme where the packaged cuts under `/usr/share/synui` pick the
  right one.
